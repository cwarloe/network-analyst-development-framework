# Developing judgment that plays out across time

Most training teaches single-moment decisions. Much technical judgment work is longitudinal — trend analysis, drift, posture over weeks, "has this been getting worse?" If a curriculum has no unit of analysis larger than one case, that gap is real and it is invisible from inside the curriculum.

**Sourcing:** distilled from a commissioned literature scan. Sources were not independently read. The medical continuity evidence and the perceptual-training effect sizes are the strongest claims and the ones most worth checking before anything expensive rests on them.

## What six fields actually do

| Field | Structure | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Medicine** | Longitudinal integrated clerkships — same patients, same supervisors, over months. Also 3–4 visit simulated longitudinal cases | **Strongest available.** LIC students meet or exceed block-rotation students on knowledge and skills, with better tolerance of uncertainty. Kirkpatrick 2–3. A dose–response pattern: more continuity, stronger effect |
| **Aviation** | Line-Oriented Flight Training — one 3–4 hour session, multiple legs, evolving conditions | Attitude change and observed behaviour change in simulators and line audits. Accident rates too rare for causal attribution |
| **Meteorology** | Continuous forecast verification against outcomes, fed back to forecasters | Observational but long-run: human forecasters beat models by 20–40% on some thresholds across decades, partly attributed to verification feedback |
| **Quality control** | Control charts, trend rules, CUSUM/EWMA for small sustained shifts | Method mathematically validated. **Training-effect evidence much weaker** |
| **Audit** | Analytical procedures across periods, required by standards | Institutionalised and regulator-backed. Training research sparse |
| **Process safety** | HRO practice, precursor and near-miss analysis, normalisation-of-deviance case work | Rich conceptually. A 2023 systematic review found **no empirically tested interventions** |

Note the pattern: the fields with the most institutional commitment to longitudinal work are not the ones with the best evidence that training it works.

## The two structures worth stealing

**A verification tracker.** Record the decision, the stated confidence, and the expected time horizon. Reveal ground truth later. Let a personal hit-and-miss record accumulate across the whole programme.

This is worth more than it looks. It is retrieval practice, feedback, and distributed practice in a single mechanism, it runs across units rather than inside one, and it gives a learner self-referenced evidence that they are improving — which is the honest form of "am I ready?" that needs nobody to score anybody. It comes from operational meteorology, not from education research.

**Multi-episode cases.** The same environment recurring across several exposures, with an explicit instruction to compare the current judgment against the earlier one. Continuity is what medicine's evidence is actually about.

**Cheap first move:** most curricula already contain accidental continuity — the same host, company, or site reused across units without anyone saying so. Naming it costs a sentence and converts coincidence into structure.

## Detecting gradual change

Genuinely hard: change blindness is worst when transitions are slow, because motion cues are absent, and the task requires encoding, holding, comparing and deciding, each of which can fail under load.

But it is trainable, in several sensory domains, at modest dose:

- Perceptual training cut just-noticeable differences by roughly half, with measurable change in early visual cortex.
- Auditory change-detection training with feedback improved sensitivity, **reduced the "nothing changed" bias**, and generalised to novel stimuli.
- Seven days of visual change-detection training produced ~50% improvement with transfer to other visual tasks.

**The middle finding is the interesting one.** The bias being corrected — defaulting to *nothing has changed* — is the disposition that longitudinal analytic work most needs corrected.

## A recommendation worth declining

Decontextualised change-detection micro-drills — dashboard pairs with subtle differences, ten to twenty trials, immediate feedback — are the obvious application of the evidence above, and they were declined in the project this came from. Two reasons, both worth reusing:

1. **The transfer is unverified.** Whether low-level perceptual gains reach complex analytic judgment under domain load is plausible and untested. The cited evidence is contrast discrimination, auditory flicker, and visual conjunction search — not reasoning about whether a signal is meaningful.
2. **It may contradict your own model.** If your capability model says tool speed and interface familiarity are not substitutes for conceptual understanding, a spot-the-difference drill is closer to the thing you excluded than the thing you measure.

If built anyway, label it an unvalidated transfer bet rather than capability development.

## Two cautions on dose

- **No minimum episode count is established.** Medicine shows dose–response but not a floor. Short simulated versions report self-rated confidence rather than measured performance.
- **Effects decay.** Aviation fatigue training produced attitude effects that faded within weeks without reinforcement. Whatever a single sitting changes is unlikely to persist unreinforced — an argument for recurrence.
