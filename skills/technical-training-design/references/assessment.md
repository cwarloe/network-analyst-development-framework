# Assessing open-ended work, and checking whether reviewers agree

For work products that are written judgments — an assessment, a diagnosis, a handoff — rather than answers with a key.

**Sourcing:** distilled from a commissioned methodology brief. Sources were not independently read. The essay-marking figures are the load-bearing ones and are worth verifying before quoting anywhere consequential.

## The distinction that matters

**Global versus analytic rating.** A global rating is one overall judgment. An analytic judgment is a set of specific observations. They behave differently and the literature treats them separately.

"Did two reviewers notice the same things?" is **not a rating problem at all.** It is *intercoder reliability* from qualitative content analysis, where the question is whether two coders apply the same codes to the same open-ended text.

That reframing matters practically: it moves the work out of a literature where the absence of a rubric is a defect, and into one where coding prose against a shared taxonomy is the normal method.

> No specialised term exists for noticing-agreement in the assessment literature specifically. The construct is borrowed from content analysis.

## What a comparison can and cannot establish

**Can:**
- Whether the review guidance is usable as an instrument at all.
- Whether reviewers differ *systematically* — much disagreement is severity or interpretation, not random error.
- Which tasks support stable judgments and which are intrinsically ambiguous.

**Cannot:**
- Trustworthy scores, mastery levels, or cut-scores. Without an explicit scale, any numeric coding is an ex post research artifact.
- Reliability coefficients comparable to rubric-based systems.
- Defensible individual-level claims. Two raters and no rubric speaks to the instrument, not to a person.

## The measures

| Level | Unit | Statistic | Answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Verdict** | One work product | Cohen's kappa over 3 categories | Sanity check: does the guidance support aligned global judgment at all |
| **Issue** | Work product × issue code, flagged or not | Krippendorff's alpha (nominal), or kappa per code | The real question: did they see the same problems |

**Jaccard similarity** per work product — overlap of the two flagged sets over their union — is a good descriptive companion because it is legible without statistics training. It is not the reliability statistic.

**Thresholds** (content-analysis conventions): ≥0.80 good; 0.667–0.80 tentative; below 0.667 insufficient for strong claims. Adopt these *stated in advance*, not as validated for your setting.

## Deriving the issue codes

Do not import a generic taxonomy. **Invert your existing review guidance.** If the guide says "look for whether the writer argued the benign explanation seriously," the code is *did not argue the benign explanation seriously*.

Reviewers write their narrative exactly as they do now, then tick which codes they believe they raised. A tick-list with no scale, no weights and no total is a taxonomy, not a rubric — this does not compromise a deliberate no-scores position.

**Stronger variant:** a second pass mapping free-text comments to the codes, so agreement rests on what was actually said rather than on self-labelling. Differently-worded observations about the same underlying flaw are exactly what self-tagging hides.

## Minimum design

- **2 reviewers** per work product, blind, no discussion until capture is complete.
- **20–30 work products**, spanning multiple cases and difficulty levels. Roughly 30 units is wanted for a stable alpha; fewer is possible with wider uncertainty.

**Be realistic about this cost.** Twenty to thirty double-reviewed work products is a research programme, not a design check. Most projects do not have it and will not for a long time.

**What to do at small n:** report the per-code table and Jaccard overlap, state n, and explicitly decline to compute a coefficient. That is a real finding about the guidance. It is not reliability and must not be written up as reliability.

## Reading a low number — decide this before you run

| Pattern | Cause | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Issue agreement high, verdict lower | **Not a problem.** Same flaws seen; different tolerance for how many flaws is too many | Accept it. Report issues noticed, not verdicts |
| Both low | Guidance under-specified, or reviewers differ in experience. A finding about the instrument | Refine the guidance, or add worked examples and a calibration conversation |
| Issue low, verdict high | Reviewers land in the same place by gestalt while noticing different things — common in general-impression marking | Signal about case design and reviewer prompts, even though verdicts look fine |
| Disagreement clusters on particular work products | **The task.** Ambiguous or under-specified prompts mark less reliably | Examine those cases qualitatively. Some are genuinely multi-solution |

**Separating rater from task effects, by eye, at any n:** a *rater* effect is one reviewer flagging consistently more or fewer issues across all products, stable across tasks. A *task* effect is agreement collapsing on specific products regardless of reviewer.

## Figures worth knowing

- Trained raters, analytic rubrics: **weighted kappa ≈ 0.79–0.83**.
- Essay marking without explicit criteria: correlations often **0.3–0.6**; Cronbach's alpha as low as **0.10–0.40** for inexperienced raters.
- Genuinely rubric-less professional assessment: results run from near-zero to moderate, **no consensus range**.

## On formative reliability

Requirements genuinely scale with stakes, and low-stakes formative use tolerates less precision. But there is no published consensus threshold, and the position in the measurement literature is *strive for as much reliability as purpose and cost allow* — not *formative means reliability does not matter*.

A no-scores stance removes the obligation to defend a score. It does not remove the obligation to show that two reviewers reading the same work are not diverging at random.
